Abstract
Life-stage comparisons can inform marine environmental assessment, but experiments measure endpoints with different biological meanings. We reanalysed 2,412 log response ratios from 162 StudyID clusters in a curated database of marine invertebrate responses to warming and acidification. Directional heterogeneity occurred among taxonomic classes, endpoint pathways and driver-specific life stages. In the primary metamorphic-taxa subset, embryos showed the clearest downward shift in measured endpoints (lnRR = -0.233, 95% confidence interval -0.367 to -0.100; q = 0.007). Endpoint alignment weakened this signal; without independent direction coding, aligned estimates remain rule-dependent sensitivity analyses. On matched stage-by-driver evidence, 13 of 27 numerical contrasts between response definitions survived false-discovery-rate correction. Twelve involved absolute magnitude and therefore provide no evidence of greater biological harm. Contrast-specific small-sample tests did not separate stages after correction. Settlement and metamorphosis were insufficient for performance inference: 10 studies contributed 41 effects, but only one study supplied five direction-interpretable effects. Sampling-error-calibrated departures were sensitive to between-study heterogeneity. A signed random-effects sensitivity model retained the negative embryo mean, while direct-outcome alignment yielded no supported stage mean. Stage estimates therefore depend on response definition and evidence composition. Environmental assessments should specify the measured outcome before comparing life stages and require demographic evidence before inferring recruitment consequences.
Keywords
Ocean warming; ocean acidification; marine invertebrates; life-history stage; response definition; evidence synthesis; meta-analysis
1. Introduction
Ocean warming and acidification alter marine environments through thermal constraints and carbonate-chemistry change (Orr et al., 2005; Doney et al., 2009; Poertner, 2010; Riebesell and Gattuso, 2015). Invertebrate life cycles connect fertilization, embryogenesis, larval development, settlement, metamorphosis and adult growth. Responses during these transitions can affect recruitment and population renewal (Thorson, 1950; Strathmann, 1985; Pechenik, 1999; Marshall and Morgan, 2011). Stage-resolved experiments can inform environmental assessment when measured outcomes are linked to the ecological processes of concern.
Experiments measure survival, growth, calcification, development, physiology and molecular traits across taxa and exposure regimes (Anthony et al., 2008; Byrne et al., 2010; Ries et al., 2009; Gazeau et al., 2013; Sun et al., 2024; Servetto et al., 2025). These endpoints differ in numerical direction and demographic relevance. A decline in survival and a decline in mortality have opposite implications. Pooling them without specifying the target quantity can produce a precise estimate with an ambiguous biological meaning.
Experimental sensitivity describes a measured response under a specified exposure. Endpoint alignment adds a rule about the biological meaning of its direction. Ecological risk additionally requires information about exposure and consequences for an assessment endpoint, such as population persistence (Suter, 2006). Absolute response magnitude preserves the extent of change while discarding its direction. None of these experimental summaries alone measures population vulnerability.
Settlement and metamorphosis illustrate the distinction. Cue detection, attachment and post-settlement survival connect dispersal to benthic recruitment (Hadfield, 2011). Experiments on settlement cues and larval survival identify plausible routes by which environmental change may affect replenishment (Talmage and Gobler, 2010; Wu et al., 2024). Demographic consequences nevertheless depend on subsequent survival and density-dependent processes. Combining settlement with broader developmental categories may obscure both this distinction and the scarcity of relevant measurements.
Recent syntheses document cancellation among oppositely directed responses and variation across taxa, traits and stressor combinations (Alter et al., 2024; Khalil et al., 2022; Bridge et al., 2024). Apparent stage differences may therefore reflect endpoint composition as well as organismal responses. Whether these differences persist when numerical direction, endpoint meaning and evidence structure are considered jointly remains unresolved. An OpenAlex context search retrieved 360 unique records published during 2000-2025. Keyword co-occurrence and activity intervals describe research coverage (Fig. 1b,d); quantitative eligibility was determined separately.
We asked three questions: how is evidence distributed across taxa, endpoint pathways and life stages; how do stage patterns change after endpoint alignment; and how much apparent magnitude is associated with a mean shift after accounting for sampling error and between-study heterogeneity? We compared directional, endpoint-aligned and absolute aggregated responses, then examined dependence, coding rules and transformation order.
2. Materials and methods
2.1 Database provenance, scope and analytical units
We conducted a secondary quantitative analysis of a curated effect-size database, supplemented by a targeted search in 2026 for studies published through 2025. The archived input version is labelled 'verified pre-2026 analysis dataset', dated 26 June 2026; the update trail continues through 28 July 2026. The retained original layer contains 2,238 effects, an earlier expansion 75, and the current update 99. The supplied records do not establish the original curator, initial creation date, complete original search or full exclusion history. This is a curated evidence synthesis rather than a reconstructed PRISMA-compliant systematic review (Page et al., 2021).
The targeted search retained sources, candidate records, exclusions and extracted rows (Supplementary Table S1; Supplementary Fig. S1). It screened 21 candidates and added 99 effects assigned to four StudyIDs. The added rows comprise 43 effects from Williams et al. (2024), 24 from Jury and Toonen (2024), 30 from Kirkland et al. (2025), and two from Sire de Vilar et al. (2024a,b). The two Sire de Vilar datasets share StudyID 246. They were therefore treated as dependent endpoints. The 99 added effects are not 99 independent experiments.
The data hierarchy is source publication or dataset, experiment where identifiable, treatment-control comparison, endpoint effect, StudyID cluster, and analysis-specific aggregate. One effect is one endpoint-specific lnRR. StudyID groups linked records for dependence control and does not count independent experiments. The 162 REF values map one-to-one to 162 StudyIDs in the full database. All 2,412 RowKeys are unique. This audit identifies exact key duplication; missing experimental identifiers still limit identification of shared samples. Supplementary Table S10 provides the reference crosswalk, row-level source dataset, source row, extraction notes and missing locators.
Full-database descriptions use 2,412 effects, 162 StudyIDs and 159 species or higher-taxon labels. Primary stage and reference-deviation analyses use taxa coded as having a metamorphic stage: 2,236 effects from 149 StudyIDs. The full and primary datasets contain 187 and 174 StudyID-by-stage cells, respectively. The primary dataset contains 442 StudyID-by-driver cells; counts of 147-148 refer to individual driver strata. We use k for effects, m for StudyID clusters and s for cells defined by stated grouping variables. Complete counts are supplied in Supplementary Table S10. Exposure intensity, duration and shared-control covariance were incompletely retained and were not imputed.
2.2 Evidence context, taxonomy and ecological strata
The OpenAlex Works API was queried on 5 September 2026 for marine-invertebrate acidification, warming and combined-exposure literature. We retained the first 200 relevance-ranked article or review records per query, restricted to 2000-2025. DOI, OpenAlex ID or title deduplication reduced 600 returns to 360 records. Keyword counts describe this retrieval sample; it was not the quantitative inclusion set. Temporal activity intervals show first, last and peak occurrence, rather than algorithmic citation bursts.
We resolved dataset labels through the World Register of Marine Species on 7 September 2026. Accepted or synonym-linked matches supplied 143 placements; 13 aggregate labels and three query-error labels remained in the audit. Of 140 unique accepted species names submitted to Open Tree of Life, 139 entered name resolution and 138 unique OTT tips entered the induced subtree retrieved on 8 September 2026 (Hinchliff et al., 2015). Cryolaternula elliptica used the documented approximate match Laternula elliptica (score 0.789). Ostrea edulis resolved to OTT 1033104 but was absent from the current synthetic subtree. Raw responses and exclusions are archived. Unit plotting branches show topology; they provide neither evolutionary distances nor a phylogenetic covariance matrix.
Observed endpoint stage and species developmental mode were coded separately (Supplementary Table S2). The primary restriction to metamorphic taxa makes the settlement comparison biologically defined. Ecological subgroup analyses additionally describe the full database and therefore use a different population from primary stage analyses. Five moderators were available: phylum, taxonomic class, adult ecological guild, endpoint category and observed stage.
Guilds are operational class-based adult habitat and mobility strata. Classes considered comparatively uniform were assigned to benthic-sessile, benthic-mobile or nektonic-swimming strata. Classes spanning contrasting modes were coded mixed-uncertain and excluded from formal guild comparisons. These include Bivalvia, Gastropoda, Malacostraca, Polychaeta, Copepoda, Scyphozoa and Nematoda. The archived rule table supplies WoRMS taxonomic links, which establish taxonomic placement but do not independently validate ecological traits. Guild analyses are therefore exploratory; adult labels do not describe every life stage. Supplementary class and guild sensitivities propagate assumed within-study correlations into aggregate variances rather than treating rho as a plotting label.
2.3 Endpoint interpretation and response definitions
Raw directional response is lnRR = ln(treatment mean/control mean). Sampling variance follows the standard response-ratio approximation (Hedges et al., 1999; Lajeunesse, 2011), using the archived model variance and variance-floor sensitivities. Negative values describe lower measured endpoint values. They acquire a biological interpretation only after the endpoint is identified.
A deterministic dictionary assigns putatively adverse metrics, including mortality, abnormality, delay, bleaching and damage, to an increasing-adverse rule. Explicit survival, transition success, reproduction, growth and direct calcification metrics receive an increasing-beneficial rule. Endpoint-aligned lnRR retains the original sign for increasing-beneficial metrics and reverses it for increasing-adverse metrics. Physiology, respiration, behaviour, mineral composition and other ambiguous metrics remain unaligned unless explicit wording supplies a direction. Growth, size, calcification and timing proxies are provisional interpretations, not validated measures of fitness. The label 'endpoint-aligned response' is used throughout for this rule-dependent target.
The dictionary retains 1,235 effects from 112 StudyIDs in the full database and 1,160 effects from 105 StudyIDs in the primary subset. Settlement/metamorphosis retains five effects from one StudyID and is insufficient for aligned inference. Independent duplicate coding was not performed, so agreement statistics are unavailable. Supplementary Table S4b supplies each metric, its direction rule, biological rationale, provisional confidence and exclusion status, with a row-level audit. We compare the existing broad and strict dictionaries and add a direct-outcome restriction covering explicit survival, mortality, reproductive output and transition success. These are coding sensitivities rather than independent validation.
Four magnitude summaries are distinguished. The principal absolute aggregated response is the absolute value of a signed StudyID-cell mean. Endpoint-level absolute response averages absolute lnRR before aggregation and is confined to sensitivity analysis. The median absolute cell mean and the noise-adjusted RMS are additional descriptive summaries (Supplementary Fig. S10). Calibrated departure subtracts an expected absolute noise contribution from an absolute cell mean (Fig. 6). Supplementary Table S11 supplies their equations and uses. None is interpreted as a direct estimate of harm.
2.4 Ecological meta-analysis and paired target comparisons
For full-database moderator tests, we inverse-variance aggregated directional effects within StudyID-by-moderator-level-by-driver cells. Levels required at least three StudyIDs; omnibus tests required at least two eligible levels. Random-intercept REML meta-regressions used StudyID-cluster sandwich-robust F tests with finite-sample adjustment. The 15 moderator-by-driver tests formed one BH-FDR family (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995). These exploratory tests describe heterogeneity in the sampled evidence, where taxonomy, endpoint and exposure may covary.
For Figure 2, all three targets use identical direction-interpretable rows within each full-database subgroup. Directional and aligned effects were inverse-variance aggregated within StudyID-by-subgroup-by-driver cells. Absolute response was calculated after signed aggregation. Separate REML models summarize each target; the same working aggregate variance is used to describe uncertainty in the folded summary. Signed-target P values are BH-adjusted within each moderator-by-target family across levels and drivers. Absolute summaries have no zero-null P value. Confidence intervals describe pooled means and prediction intervals describe comparable future study cells under the model.
Figure 3 restricts the matched-target analysis to metamorphic taxa and StudyID-by-stage-by-driver cells. Within each cell, we calculate aligned minus directional, absolute minus directional and absolute minus aligned. Mean paired differences give each StudyID cell equal weight, whereas the separate REML summaries use model-based weights. Thus a paired mean difference need not equal the difference between two REML means. Percentile intervals use 9,999 bootstrap resamples of complete paired cells. Two-sided centered-null bootstrap P values use the archived +1 correction and BH adjustment only for the nine signed aligned-minus-directional contrasts. Folded absolute contrasts are reported as descriptive paired differences because their differences are structurally non-negative.
Figure 4 applies the same paired procedure to ecological subgroups across the full database. Its 216 estimable contrasts form a separate BH family. The family boundaries were specified for these exploratory analyses and are recorded in code; the study was not prospectively registered. Tests involving absolute response quantify a change in numerical target after folding, not biological damage or a stage-to-stage difference. No inferential estimate is reported for fewer than three StudyID cells. All estimates, effect counts, cell counts, P values and q values are supplied in source tables.
2.5 Dependence, stage contrasts and small-study diagnostics
The primary stage model uses unaggregated effects and correlated-effects robust variance estimation with StudyID clusters (Hedges et al., 2010). We fit no-intercept directional and endpoint-aligned models at assumed correlations rho = 0, 0.5 and 0.8, with small-sample corrections. Stage means retain the established RVE coefficient estimates. Pairwise stage tests now use CR2 covariance and contrast-specific Satterthwaite degrees of freedom through rank-one HTZ tests in clubSandwich. Omnibus stage equality uses the corresponding multiple-constraint HTZ F test (Pustejovsky and Tipton, 2018). BH adjustment is applied separately to stage means and estimable pairwise contrasts within each target and rho.
Conventional sensitivities aggregate within StudyID-by-stage cells before REML estimation with metafor (Borenstein et al., 2010; Viechtbauer, 2010). They report heterogeneity and prediction intervals on that aggregated scale (Higgins and Thompson, 2002). Additional checks restrict endpoints to Development, vary variance floors, compare all taxa with metamorphic taxa, and omit one StudyID at a time. Alternative StudyID, species and family clustering uses the same family-complete subset of 146 StudyIDs; StudyIDs 229, 234 and 235 are excluded only from that comparison.
Egger-type regressions use one signed aggregate per StudyID within each stage and require at least 10 StudyIDs (Egger et al., 1997). Their precision plots assess small-study asymmetry. Endpoint composition, heterogeneity and dependence can influence these diagnostics, so they do not isolate selective publication. Figure 5d additionally displays StudyID-by-stage-by-driver cells; these plotted points can share StudyID and are descriptive. Non-significant stage tests are interpreted as insufficient corrected evidence for separation, without claiming equivalence or a universal hierarchy.
2.6 Reference-deviation calibration and signed-distribution sensitivity
For cell i, let a_i = sum_j(w_ij lnRR_ij)/sum_j(w_ij), where w_ij = 1/v_ij. Absolute departure is D_i = |a_i|. Under a signed zero-effect normal model, its expected sampling contribution is sqrt(2v_i/pi). The calibrated descriptive index E_i subtracts this expectation from D_i. It is a noise-offset index, not an unbiased latent-effect estimator. Cells are defined by StudyID crossed with stage, driver, endpoint category, or stage and driver, according to the analysis.
The archived calibration compares weighted absolute cell means with 1,200 simulations from N(0,v_i), using BH correction across evidence-set and grouping combinations. Bootstrap intervals use 900 cell resamples. A sensitivity calibration adds a stage-specific DerSimonian-Laird heterogeneity estimate and simulates N(0,v_i + tau-squared). This deliberately conservative, zero-centred benchmark asks how much departure exceeds both sampling variation and unexplained heterogeneity. Its null parameters are estimated from the same evidence, so it is a descriptive model check rather than an independent validation test.
Noise-adjusted RMS is sqrt(max(mean(a_i squared - v_i),0)). The subtraction is motivated by E(a_i squared) = theta_i squared + v_i under unbiased signed sampling. Truncation enforces a non-negative summary but creates a point mass at zero and changes its sampling properties. We report negative cell contributions, truncated point estimates, and truncation frequencies across 4,000 bootstrap resamples, alongside untruncated squared-moment intervals (Supplementary Table S12).
To evaluate magnitude without fitting a model to folded observations, we additionally fit signed normal random-effects models a_i ~ N(mu,tau-squared + v_i) by REML, with Hartung-Knapp intervals. Their implied latent absolute mean is tau sqrt(2/pi) exp[-mu squared/(2 tau-squared)] + mu[2 Phi(mu/tau)-1], with limit |mu| when tau = 0. We compare this with the centred value tau sqrt(2/pi). These are model-implied descriptive quantities. Using signed data preserves direction and avoids discarding information to fit a folded-normal likelihood. We also recompute aggregate variances at within-cell correlations of 0, 0.5 and 0.8; the weights defining a_i remain inverse marginal variances. Supplementary Figure S11 and Table S12 report all specifications. These additions were conducted during revision and are exploratory.
3. Results
3.1 Evidence composition and coverage
The full database contains 419 embryo, 472 larval, 41 settlement/metamorphosis and 1,480 juvenile/adult effects (Fig. 1). Primary metamorphic-taxa counts are 406, 472, 41 and 1,317 effects from 25, 38, 10 and 101 StudyIDs, respectively. These stage counts overlap: 127 of the 149 StudyIDs measured one stage, 19 measured two and three measured three. Most stage comparisons therefore combine different experiments rather than following the same organisms across a life cycle.
All settlement/metamorphosis effects belong to Development and represent 11 species or higher-taxon labels. Only five effects from one StudyID met the direction dictionary. The greater coverage of juvenile/adult endpoints measures evidence availability, not biological importance. Taxonomic resolution yielded 143 WoRMS placements, and the Open Tree subtree retained 138 unique OTT tips (Fig. 1a). Unresolved and aggregate labels remain in the provenance audit.
3.2 Moderator heterogeneity
All-endpoint directional moderator tests and matched-target subgroup estimates answer different questions. The former describe heterogeneity across the available evidence (Supplementary Fig. S9); the latter hold eligible rows constant within a subgroup when comparing definitions (Fig. 2). Full-database analyses are reported here, followed by primary stage models restricted to metamorphic taxa.
Directional heterogeneity among classes was supported under acidification (F(8,139) = 7.77, q < 0.001) and warming (F(8,139) = 4.68, q < 0.001). The combined-exposure result did not survive correction (F(8,140) = 2.24, q = 0.052). Phylum tests were unsupported under all drivers (q >= 0.175). Class estimates and counts are tabulated in Supplementary Figure S7 source data; Figure 2a displays the smaller direction-interpretable subset.
Benthic-mobile and benthic-sessile classes supplied the formally comparable guild strata. Mixed-uncertain classes were excluded from guild tests, and nektonic-swimming classes had only two StudyIDs per driver. No eligible guild comparison survived correction (acidification q = 0.770; warming and combined exposure q = 0.331). Within-guild mean responses should therefore not be interpreted as evidence of between-guild differences (Fig. 2c; Supplementary Fig. S8).
Endpoint pathway was heterogeneous under acidification (F(8,151) = 3.11, q = 0.009), warming (F(8,151) = 4.19, q < 0.001) and combined exposure (F(8,152) = 2.35, q = 0.044). Life-stage heterogeneity was supported under warming (F(3,156) = 27.18, q < 0.001) and combined exposure (F(3,157) = 3.80, q = 0.029), but not acidification (q = 0.758). These all-endpoint, driver-specific tests differ in population and dependence model from the pooled primary RVE tests below. Exact k, m, s and model degrees of freedom accompany each omnibus test in Supplementary Table S9.
Across the five moderators, 216 of 306 subgroup-by-driver-by-target combinations met the minimum of three matched StudyID cells. Figure 2 shows class, endpoint, guild and stage estimates; phylum results remain in the source table. The separate paired analysis contained 216 estimable contrasts, 80 of which survived its BH correction (Fig. 4). The matched subset excludes context-dependent endpoints, so its endpoint panel should not be read as an estimate for every physiological pathway in the full database.
3.3 Stage-level directional and endpoint-aligned response
The primary RVE embryo estimate was -0.233 (95% CI -0.367 to -0.100; k = 406, m = 25; q = 0.007) at rho = 0.8. It describes a downward shift in measured endpoints. Larval, settlement/metamorphosis and juvenile/adult means had intervals crossing zero. Estimates changed by less than 0.001 across tested rho values (Fig. 5a; Supplementary Table S13).
CR2 stage equality was unsupported for directional response (HTZ F(3,26.05) = 2.01, P = 0.137). None of six pairwise contrasts survived BH correction (Fig. 5c). A significant embryo mean against zero therefore did not establish a supported difference from another stage.
The endpoint-aligned embryo mean was -0.075 (95% CI -0.252 to 0.102; k = 247, m = 17; q = 0.558). The larval mean was -0.070 (95% CI -0.188 to 0.048; k = 250, m = 30; q = 0.558). Juvenile/adult estimates also crossed zero; settlement/metamorphosis was not estimable. Aligned stage equality was unsupported (HTZ F(2,30.51) = 0.836, P = 0.443), as were the three corrected pairwise contrasts. These rule-dependent results weaken a general performance interpretation of the raw embryo signal.
3.4 Matched evidence and numerical target differences
Within nine estimable primary stage-by-driver combinations, matched directional means ranged from -0.233 to -0.022; five survived BH correction. Endpoint-aligned means ranged from -0.075 to 0.058, with none supported. Absolute aggregated means ranged from 0.093 to 0.253 (Fig. 3a). All three targets used identical eligible cells within each combination. Settlement/metamorphosis supplied only one such cell per driver and was not estimated.
Thirteen of 27 paired numerical contrasts survived BH correction (Fig. 3b). The only supported aligned-minus-directional contrast occurred for embryos under combined exposure (difference 0.162, 95% bootstrap CI 0.043 to 0.312; s = 16; q = 0.037). Seven absolute-minus-directional and five absolute-minus-aligned contrasts were significant. Thus, 12 of the 13 supported differences involved folding; these are changes in target definition rather than evidence of greater biological harm. Full effect and cell counts are provided with the estimates in Supplementary Table S9.
Point-estimate orderings also depended on eligible endpoints. The largest absolute aggregated estimate occurred in settlement/metamorphosis for all endpoints, larvae after restriction to Development, and juvenile/adult endpoints in the direction-interpretable subset (Supplementary Fig. S10; Supplementary Table S6). These descriptive orderings were not established as statistically separated ranks. Averaging endpoint-level absolute responses increased magnitude relative to folding after signed aggregation, because opposite responses could no longer cancel.
3.5 Sensitivity of the embryo directional estimate
Restriction to Development retained a negative pooled embryo estimate (-0.149, 95% CI -0.259 to -0.039; k = 90, m = 11). Figure 5b shows its driver-specific counterparts. Alternative StudyID, species and family clustering retained negative embryo intervals in the family-complete dataset (Supplementary Table S7). Leave-one-StudyID-out REML means ranged from -0.133 to -0.109. These checks show directional consistency across the tested specifications.
The additional direct-outcome alignment gave embryo, larval and juvenile/adult means of -0.096, -0.070 and 0.027, respectively. Their 95% intervals were -0.315 to 0.124, -0.448 to 0.308, and -0.066 to 0.120. Counts were k = 123, 69 and 73 and m = 10, 13 and 15; all BH q values were 0.674 (Supplementary Table S12). Restricting alignment to explicit outcomes therefore did not yield a supported stage mean. This sensitivity does not substitute for independent endpoint coding.
The embryo precision diagnostic showed small-study asymmetry (Egger-type z = -2.94, P = 0.003; m = 25; Supplementary Fig. S4). Each regression observation was one StudyID aggregate. The result is compatible with several sources of asymmetry, including endpoint composition and heterogeneity; it does not isolate publication bias.
3.6 Reference-deviation and signed-distribution sensitivity
Weighted absolute departures were 0.067, 0.076, 0.572 and 0.100 for embryo, larval, settlement/metamorphosis and juvenile/adult endpoints (Fig. 6a). Sampling-error simulations yielded P = 1/1201 for each stage. After adding between-study heterogeneity, the calibrated descriptive indices were -0.025, -0.020, 0.043 and -0.041; all adjusted probabilities were 1.00. A positive absolute departure therefore did not establish a non-zero directional shift beyond the heterogeneity benchmark.
The signed REML sensitivity gave an embryo mean of -0.127 (95% Hartung-Knapp CI -0.197 to -0.058; k = 406, m = 25; q = 0.004) at within-cell rho = 0. Its negative interval persisted at rho = 0.5 and 0.8 (Supplementary Fig. S11a). For settlement/metamorphosis, the signed mean was -0.042 (95% CI -0.363 to 0.279; k = 41, m = 10; q = 0.774). Its implied latent absolute mean was 0.356, compared with 0.355 under the fitted zero-centred heterogeneity distribution. This near equality attributes most model-implied magnitude to dispersion rather than a common directional shift.
None of 33 eligible RMS point estimates was truncated to zero, but bootstrap truncation reached 11.1% for settlement/metamorphosis at rho = 0.8 and 9.3% for aligned-subset larvae. At rho = 0 in the all-endpoint primary subset, 3/25 embryo, 5/38 larval, 4/10 settlement and 20/101 juvenile/adult cells contributed negative noise-adjusted squared terms. These results expose boundary sensitivity that the positive RMS point estimates alone conceal (Supplementary Fig. S11b; Supplementary Table S12).
4. Discussion
4.1 Biological interpretation of the stage pattern
Embryo endpoints showed the clearest downward directional shift, but alignment weakened the pooled estimate and corrected stage contrasts did not establish separation. The pattern is compatible with the sensitivity of early development described in experimental reviews (Byrne and Przeslawski, 2013; Przeslawski et al., 2015). This synthesis cannot identify whether developmental physiology, endpoint composition, exposure intensity or taxonomic structure produced it. Mechanistic interpretations therefore remain hypotheses rather than measured explanations.
Three response dimensions matter for environmental assessment: measured direction, endpoint-aligned change and departure from a reference. Ecological risk assessment requires a specified assessment endpoint and evidence linking measurements to consequences (Suter, 2006). Adverse-outcome pathways similarly distinguish molecular or physiological responses from adverse organismal outcomes (Ankley et al., 2010). When ecological damage or recruitment is the question, we favour specifying biologically justified endpoint directions before synthesis. That preference depends on endpoint-specific validation; it is not evidence that the present dictionary measures harm more accurately.
The paired analyses show how numerical results change under alignment and folding on matched evidence. Twelve of the 13 supported primary contrasts involved the absolute target, so their significance mainly demonstrates transformation sensitivity. The signed-distribution analysis adds a biological caution: a large absolute response can coexist with a small mean shift when study effects vary in direction. Comparing those summaries as if they were interchangeable measures of damage would obscure this distinction.
4.2 Ecological context of heterogeneity
Class and endpoint-pathway heterogeneity identify where pooled evidence is least homogeneous. Phylum and adult guild did not show corrected heterogeneity, while the eligible guild analysis covered only two comparatively uniform benthic strata. This result has limited relevance to pelagic or nektonic organisms and to ecological transitions within a species. Adult guild assignments provide a reproducible description of evidence concentration, rather than a comprehensive functional classification.
Endpoint composition connects this heterogeneity to ecological questions. Survival measures persistence of individuals over the experimental interval; calcification measures skeletal production; respiration measures metabolic activity. Their links to population performance differ. For example, increased respiration could reflect compensation or physiological demand, whereas reduced survival directly records fewer surviving individuals. The current exposure fields are too incomplete to distinguish these mechanisms statistically. Taxon, endpoint and driver-specific estimates are consequently most useful for designing comparable experiments.
4.3 Settlement, metamorphosis and recruitment evidence
Settlement and metamorphosis connect planktonic dispersal with entry into benthic populations (Hadfield, 2011). Effects on settlement cues offer one plausible pathway linking environmental change to this transition (Wu et al., 2024). In the present database, however, 10 StudyIDs contribute 41 developmental effects and only one contributes five direction-interpretable effects. The evidence is insufficient for aligned performance or recruitment inference. The large absolute estimate is also compatible with substantial study heterogeneity.
A research priority is to measure settlement success, metamorphic completion and early post-settlement survival together under matched exposures. These outcomes would help establish demographic links that generic developmental, biomarker or metabolic endpoints leave unresolved. This is a proposed design improvement, not a monitoring benefit validated by the present data.
4.4 Environmental assessment implications
The practical contribution is an endpoint-specific interpretation framework. A directional summary can identify consistent changes worth investigating; an aligned summary requires a justified biological rule; an absolute summary describes departures that may warrant follow-up. Monitoring programmes should record which of these questions a metric addresses. Connections to recruitment, population persistence or ecosystem services require additional demographic and field evidence.
Priority setting should therefore combine exposure information with endpoint-specific consequences and evidence coverage. Sparse settlement evidence warrants targeted measurement, while dense juvenile/adult evidence should not be treated as proof of greater biological importance. Reporting exposure duration, temperature, carbonate chemistry, developmental substage and shared-control identifiers would make subsequent syntheses more informative for environmental assessment.
4.5 Limitations
Evidence provenance is incomplete for the inherited database. The targeted update and row-level crosswalk improve traceability, but comprehensive search coverage, original exclusions and shared experimental samples remain partly unresolved. Generalization is restricted to the curated, extractable evidence.
Endpoint alignment depends on a deterministic dictionary without independent duplicate coding. The direct-outcome, strict and broad sensitivities quantify some rule dependence; they do not validate growth, calcification or timing proxies as fitness outcomes. Aligned results remain sensitivity analyses, and settlement/metamorphosis is particularly underrepresented.
Dependence and heterogeneity also limit interpretation. Unknown shared-control covariance and exposure differences persist after clustering and aggregation, and the species topology is not used as a covariance model. Point-estimate orderings are descriptive; non-significant contrasts do not establish equivalence. Combined-stressor differences are likewise descriptive because the covariance needed for an interaction test was unavailable (Supplementary Fig. S6).
5. Conclusions
Stage-level experimental responses depend on endpoint definition and evidence composition. The embryo directional estimate was negative across the tested specifications, while endpoint-aligned results remained rule-dependent sensitivity analyses without a supported stage mean. Corrected CR2 contrasts did not separate stages. Most significant paired target differences involved absolute transformation and should not be interpreted as biological harm.
Settlement and metamorphosis remain insufficient for performance inference: only one of 10 contributing studies supplied direction-interpretable effects. Environmental assessment will benefit from comparable outcome definitions, explicit exposure reporting and experiments linking settlement, survival and recruitment. The present results support these evidence requirements rather than a population-vulnerability ranking.
